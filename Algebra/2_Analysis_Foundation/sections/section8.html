8. The Chain Rule

An Introduction to the Chain Rule

When finding the rate of change for nested functions, you cannot find the overall derivative by simply differentiating the parts independently. Think of it like a chain reaction: changing the inside variable shifts the inner layer, which then forces the outer function to change as well.

💡 The Outside-In Mental Model:

Think of a composite function like a wrapped gift. To find how fast the total system changes, you use a two-step process:
Step 1: Differentiate the Outside Wrapper (leaving the contents untouched).
Step 2: Multiply by the rate of change of the Inside Contents.

The Chain Rule Definition

If a composite function is defined as h(x) = f(g(x)), its derivative is found by multiplying the rate of change of the outer function by the rate of change of the inner function:

h'(x) = f'(g(x)) · g'(x)

🧮 Leibniz Notation Proof

Let y = f(u) and u = g(x). A small change in x forces a change in the inner expression u, which then causes a change in the final output y:

ΔyΔx = ΔyΔu · ΔuΔx

If we take the limit as the change in x approaches zero (Δx → 0), these small steps turn into exact derivatives:

dydx = dydu · dudx
🚨 The Composite Trap — Forgetting the Inner Part:

The derivative of a nested function is never equal to just the derivative of the outside layer on its own: [f(g(x))]' ≠ f'(g(x)). Forgetting to multiply by the inner derivative g'(x) is a very common error that will lead to completely incorrect tangent lines and answers.

Operational Formula Matrix

In practice, calculating derivatives using the abstract composite formula can take a lot of time. Instead, we use optimized shortcut patterns to handle standard algebraic expressions instantly.

🌍 THE UNIVERSAL NESTING PROTOCOL:

While the two patterns below are the most common algebraic expressions you will face, the Chain Rule works exactly the same for any nested function. Whether you have an algebraic expression inside a trigonometric function sin(g(x)), an exponential layer eg(x), or a logarithm ln(g(x)), you always differentiate from the outside-in: find the derivative of the outer function first, then multiply by the inner derivative g'(x).

📦 PROFILE A: POWERS OF BRACKETS

When an expression inside brackets is raised to a power:

[(g(x))n]' = n · (g(x))n-1 · g'(x)
  1. Bring the power exponent n to the very front.
  2. Decrease the bracket's power layer by exactly 1.
  3. Multiply everything by the internal derivative g'(x).
🧪 PROFILE B: RADICAL RADIX CORES

When a variable expression is nested underneath a classic square root sign:

√g(x)' = g'(x) 2√g(x)
  1. Compute the clean internal derivative g'(x).
  2. Drop that derivative directly into the numerator position.
  3. Set the denominator to exactly two times the original parent root.

Let the changes in our variables approach zero. We can evaluate these steps by multiplying our fraction by a matching fraction containing our middle variable, u:

ΔyΔx = ΔyΔu · ΔuΔx

Because the inner function layer g(x) is perfectly continuous, forcing the change in x to zero (Δx → 0) automatically forces the change in the middle variable u to zero as well (Δu → 0):

limΔx→0 ΔyΔx = (limΔu→0 ΔyΔu) · (limΔx→0 ΔuΔx)

These individual limits turn smoothly into standard derivative formulas, proving exactly why the derivative of the outer wrapper multiplies by the inner derivative part:

dydx = dydu · dudx

Chain Rule Examples

Take a look at how the Chain Rule handles basic nested expressions, and how it scales up when combined with the product and quotient rules. Following these problems step-by-step will help you identify the inside and outside layers clearly.

Example 8.1:

Differentiate the following composite function using the Chain Rule:

h(x) = √3x2 - 5x + 2
Solution Steps:
  1. Step 1: Identify and differentiate the inner and outer functions
    We separate our nested function into its two parts, identifying the inside function g(x) and the outside function f(u):
    Inside function: g(x) = 3x2 - 5x + 2 → g'(x) = 6x - 5
    Outside function: f(u) = √u = u1/2 → f'(u) = 12√u
  2. Step 2: Multiply the derivatives together
    We substitute our parts directly into the Chain Rule formula, keeping the original inside function g(x) inside the outside derivative. Then we multiply by the inside derivative g'(x):
    h'(x) = f'(g(x)) · g'(x) = 12√3x2 - 5x + 2 · (6x - 5)
    Write down the final derivative as a single combined fraction expression:
    h'(x) = 6x - 52√3x2 - 5x + 2
Example 8.2:

Find the derivative function f'(x) for the given bracket function:

f(x) = (4x3 - 7)5
Solution Steps:
  1. Step 1: Identify the inside and outside parts
    We find our inside expression and our outside power layer:
    Inside function part: g(x) = 4x3 - 7 → g'(x) = 12x2
    Outside power part: u5 → 5u4
  2. Step 2: Apply the power rule to the bracket and multiply
    We bring the main exponent forward, decrease its power by one, and multiply the expression directly by the inside derivative:
    f'(x) = 5 · (g(x))4 · g'(x) = 5 · (4x3 - 7)4 · (12x2)
  3. Step 3: Multiply the numbers to simplify the answer
    We multiply our front numbers together to finish our final algebraic answer:
    f'(x) = (5 · 12x2) · (4x3 - 7)4 = 60x2 · (4x3 - 7)4
Example 8.3:

Find the derivative of this function containing an expression inside a square root using the Product Rule and Chain Rule together:

h(x) = x · √2x + 1
Solution Steps:
  1. Step 1: Separate the function and apply the Chain Rule to the nested part
    Identify the two main product parts: u(x) = x and the nested root part v(x) = √2x + 1. Differentiate both:
    First part: u(x) = x → u'(x) = 1
    Second part (Chain Rule): Inside is g(x) = 2x + 1 → g'(x) = 2. Outside is f(w) = √w.
    v'(x) = 12√2x + 1 · 2 = 1√2x + 1
  2. Step 2: Substitute components into the Product Rule formula
    Assemble the fragments using the structure u'v + uv':
    h'(x) = (1) · √2x + 1 + x · 1√2x + 1
  3. Step 3: Find a common denominator to simplify the final fraction
    Multiply the first term by √2x + 1 / √2x + 1 to combine them into a single unified term:
    h'(x) = (2x + 1) + x√2x + 1
    h'(x) = 3x + 1√ 2x + 1
Example 8.4:

Find the derivative of this fraction containing brackets raised to a power in the denominator using the Quotient Rule and Chain Rule together:

h(x) = x(3x - 2)2
Solution Steps:
  1. Step 1: Separate the top and bottom parts and apply the Chain Rule
    We let our top numerator expression be u(x) = x and our bottom denominator expression be the nested power function v(x) = (3x - 2)2. We find their derivatives separately:
    Numerator part: u(x) = x → u'(x) = 1
    Denominator part (Chain Rule): Inside expression is g(x) = 3x - 2 → g'(x) = 3. Outside power layer is w2 → 2w1.
    v'(x) = 2 \cdot (3x - 2)1 \cdot g'(x) = 2(3x - 2) \cdot (3) = 6(3x - 2)
  2. Step 2: Substitute the components into the Quotient Rule template
    We assemble our pieces directly into the standard template formula u'v - uv'v2:
    h'(x) = (1) · (3x - 2)2 - x · [6(3x - 2)][ (3x - 2)2 ]2
  3. Step 3: Factor out shared binomial terms to simplify the answer
    Instead of expanding everything out, we pull out the common binomial layer (3x - 2) from both terms in the numerator so we can cancel it cleanly with the denominator:
    h'(x) = (3x - 2) · [ (3x - 2) - 6x ](3x - 2)4 = (3x - 2) - 6x(3x - 2)3
    We combine our like terms on top to find our final simplified algebraic answer:
    h'(x) = -3x - 2(3x - 2)3
