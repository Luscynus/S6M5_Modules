8. The Chain Rule

Architectural Overview

When computing rates of change for nested systems, you cannot find the overall derivative simply by differentiating the parts independently. Imagine a domino ripple effect: changing the deepest variable shifts the inner layer, which then forces the outer wrapper to change.

💡 The Outside-In Mental Model:

Think of a composite function like a wrapped gift. To find how fast the total system changes, you use a two-step process:
Step 1: Differentiate the Outside Wrapper (leaving the contents untouched).
Step 2: Multiply by the rate of change of the Inside Contents.

The Chain Rule Definition

If a composite function is defined as h(x) = f(g(x)), its derivative is found by multiplying the rate of change of the outer layer by the rate of change of the inner layer:

h'(x) = f'(g(x)) · g'(x)

🧮 Leibniz Derivation Proof

Let y = f(u) and u = g(x). A minor fractional step adjustment Δx forces an immediate rate shift in the inner layer Δu, which ripples directly into the final system output landmark Δy:

ΔyΔx = ΔyΔu · ΔuΔx

Taking the limit at the calculus horizon as step spacing shrinks entirely to zero (Δx → 0) transforms these delta step transformations into exact algebraic differential rate identities:

dydx = dydu · dudx
🚨 The Composite Trap — Overlooking Inner Chains:

The derivative of a nested function is never equal to the derivative of the outside layer on its own: [f(g(x))]' ≠ f'(g(x)). Forgetting to multiply by the inner loop derivative multiplier g'(x) completely breaks your calculation velocity when finding physics optimizations or geometric tangent lines down the line.
